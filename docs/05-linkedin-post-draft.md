## Version 2 — Long-form article

**A $4.3B Private Equity Firm Has Zero Social Media Presence. I Spent 30 Minutes With a Partner to Understand Why.**

For my *Marketing of Financial Services* course project at Xavier Institute of Social Service, Ranchi, I expected to study campaigns, content calendars, and digital funnels. Instead I got 30 minutes with **Sunil Thakur, Partner at Quadria Capital**, and walked away rethinking what "marketing" even means once you leave retail finance.

Quadria manages over **US$4.3 billion** across healthcare investments in South and Southeast Asia. It has no Instagram. No LinkedIn ad campaign. No influencer partnerships. And according to Sunil, that's not an oversight — it's the model.

**Quick snapshot: Quadria Capital**
- Founded 2012, by Abrar Mir and Dr. Amit Varma
- HQ in Singapore, office in New Delhi
- Focus: healthcare exclusively — hospitals, pharma, diagnostics, medtech, health-tech — across South & SE Asia
- AUM: US$4.3B+
- Target companies: mid-market, revenue roughly INR 400–4,000 crore, profitable and cash-generating, not startups
- Flagship portfolio example: AIG Hospitals, Hyderabad

**How a private equity investment actually works**

Before I could analyze Quadria's "marketing," I had to understand what a PE firm actually does — because the process itself turns out to be inseparable from how trust gets built.

First, fundraising: the firm raises capital from Limited Partners — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, family offices — who commit for 7–10 years, in exchange for a management fee plus carried interest. Then deal sourcing to close: screening roughly 100 opportunities down to 3–5 closed deals a year, through deep due diligence and an Investment Committee vote. Then value creation and exit: 5–7 years of operational and governance work inside the portfolio company, before an IPO, strategic sale, or secondary sale returns capital to LPs — which becomes the pitch for the next fundraise.

**The question that flipped my assignment**

When I told Sunil I couldn't find Quadria anywhere on social media, his answer reframed the entire project. Digital marketing, he explained, is built for retail audiences — it doesn't translate to an institutional, enterprise-facing business. Quadria's actual visibility comes from industry conferences, LP roadshows, network events, and referrals. It's what he called a "highly reference-driven market."

The implication took me a while to sit with: in this world, **the previous deal is the marketing campaign.** An LP doesn't commit capital because of a well-timed post — they commit because a peer institution vouched for the firm, or a founder who worked with Quadria told another founder. Reputation compounds, the same way capital does.

**Marketing through the 7Ps lens**

Mapping Quadria against the classic 7Ps framework shows where institutional finance diverges hardest from retail: Promotion shifts from ads and campaigns to conferences and referral networks. People shift from relationship managers selling someone else's product to partners whose own clinical and financial expertise *is* the product. And Physical Evidence shifts from a bank statement to a track record of exits like AIG Hospitals.

**Why "narrow" beats "broad"**

Quadria's exclusive healthcare focus isn't a constraint — it's the core strategic bet the whole firm is built on. It compounds through three advantages: credibility with founders who trust deep specialists over generalists, proprietary deal flow from sector-specific network effects, and sharper LP differentiation in a fundraising market crowded with hundreds of competing funds.

**The metrics paradox**

One of the more interesting non-answers I got: asked what key metrics Quadria uses to measure its marketing strategy, Sunil was direct — there isn't a specific set of metrics for this. In a reference-driven market, the "metric" is closer to reputation itself, which doesn't reduce cleanly to a dashboard.

**Career advice for students**

Asked what he'd tell students aiming for finance or private equity, Sunil's advice was refreshingly non-technical at its core: build strong fundamentals in corporate finance and valuation, but study businesses broadly beyond healthcare or PE to develop transferable judgment. And critically — be good at storytelling and articulation. In a field where the "product" is trust and the "channel" is a conversation in a conference hallway, communication skill isn't a soft add-on — it's the delivery mechanism for everything else.

**Key takeaways**
- Institutional marketing is relationship architecture, not communication
- Sector specialization is a moat, not a limitation
- Frameworks like the 7Ps, PESTLE, and SWOT are lenses, not prescriptions
- Not every business measures marketing quantitatively — and that's not a gap, it's a signal about what kind of trust that market runs on

Grateful to Sunil Thakur and Quadria Capital for the time, and to Professor Prashant Kumar Jha at XISS Ranchi for a project brief that pushed me to go looking for marketing in a place most people wouldn't think to check.
